Between the Norman Conquest and 1516, English kings licensed markets in their thousands, each one a bet that a village would become a town. I followed all {{counts.market_places|int}} medieval market places in England and Wales to today. Fewer than a third are towns. Most are villages, and a few are fields or sea.

In {{examples.Ravenserodd.first|raw}} a market was granted at Ravenserodd, a port on the sandbanks at the mouth of the Humber. It was a borough, with {{examples.Ravenserodd.markets|words}} markets and a fair on record. The sea took it in the fourteenth century, and today there is no named settlement within 1.5 km of where it stood.

Ravenserodd is the dramatic case. The ordinary case is quieter: a village with a church, a pub and a charter in the rolls saying that, seven or eight hundred years ago, its lord was allowed to hold a market there every Tuesday. Most of England's medieval markets ended up like that. This post is about how many, and why some places made it when others did not.

My source is Samantha Letters's Online Gazetteer of Markets and Fairs in England and Wales to 1516, a superb piece of scholarship that lists, county by county, every market and fair with evidence before 1516. I turned it into numbers: {{counts.places|int}} places, {{counts.markets|int}} markets and {{counts.fairs|int}} fairs, each place with a grid reference, the kind of evidence (royal charter, other grant, or custom) and the first year a market is recorded. Then I looked every place up on today's map. This is the third of my "is it destiny?" studies, after Domesday Destiny and Minted.

A boom, then the Black Death

The Gazetteer records {{counts.market_lines_granted|int}} royal grants of a market, {{counts.market_lines_charter|int}} of them by charter. {{grants.share_1200_1349|pct0}} fall between 1200 and 1348. The busiest decade was the {{grants.peak_decade|raw}}s, with {{grants.peak_n|int}} market grants. Then plague arrived in 1348, and the boom ended almost overnight: {{grants.d1340s|int}} grants in the 1340s, {{grants.d1350s|int}} in the 1350s, and an average of under ten a decade for the rest of the period.

What they are now

To find out what each place is today I matched it to Ordnance Survey's open gazetteer of place names, which classes every settlement as a city, town, village, hamlet or suburb, and checked the borderline cases against Census 2021 built-up areas. Of {{counts.market_places|int}} market places, {{outcomes.town.n|int}} ({{outcomes.town.share|pct0}}) are towns or cities. Another {{outcomes.absorbed.n|int}} have been swallowed by a bigger town, like Croydon or Kingston upon Thames in London. {{outcomes.village.n|int}} ({{outcomes.village.share|pct0}}) are villages, {{outcomes.hamlet.n|int}} are hamlets and {{outcomes.gone.n|int}} have gone from the map altogether.

When a place got its market mattered a lot. Places with a market by custom, usually old Anglo-Saxon or Norman boroughs, did best: {{period.prescriptive.town_share|pct0}} are towns today. For places whose first market was a royal grant, the odds fall with every half century: {{period.1200 to 1249.town_share|pct0}} for grants in 1200 to 1249, {{period.1250 to 1299.town_share|pct0}} in 1250 to 1299 and just {{period.1300 to 1348.town_share|pct0}} in 1300 to 1348.

A third of a day's walk

Why did the late markets fail? Mostly because they were squeezed in. The thirteenth-century law book known as Bracton says a reasonable day's journey is twenty miles, split into three: the morning to walk to market, the middle to buy and sell, and the evening to walk home. A new market within a third of that, six and two-thirds miles or about {{spacing.bracton_km|km1}} km, could be challenged as a nuisance by an older one.

The rule did not stop the Crown. Of {{spacing.granted_n|int}} places whose first market was a grant, {{spacing.granted_close_share|pct0}} lay within {{spacing.bracton_km|km1}} km of a market already on record; the median gap was {{spacing.granted_median_earlier_km|km1}} km. But the squeezed-in markets rarely grew: {{spacing.close_town|pct0}} are towns today, against {{spacing.far_town|pct0}} of the ones with room around them.

What made a winner

Put everything into one model and a clear picture emerges. Being a borough multiplies the odds of being a town today by about {{or_all.borough.odds_ratio|f1}}. A mint, a market by custom, a fair, a major river within a kilometre and a Roman road all help too. An older market within Bracton's distance roughly halves the odds. Size in Domesday Book helps only at the very top: {{domesday_bands.4.town_share|pct0}} of market places with 100 or more households in 1086 are towns today, but below that size made little difference.

Decided by 1600

The Gazetteer also flags the places on Alan Everitt's list of market towns around 1600, which gives a halfway check. {{everitt.n|int}} medieval market places were still market towns then, and {{everitt.town_share|pct0}} of them are towns today. Of the {{everitt.not_n|int}} that had already dropped out, only {{everitt.not_town_share|pct0}} are. Most of the sorting had happened by the time of Elizabeth I. A few fell later: Lavenham in Suffolk, a market town around 1600, is a village of {{examples.Lavenham.pop|int}} people now.

The ones that came to nothing

The saddest list is the {{failed_boroughs_n|int}} boroughs, places granted urban privileges, that are now hamlets or nothing at all. Ravenserodd and Old Winchelsea were lost to the sea. Old Sarum was abandoned for Salisbury. Castell y Bere, Dinefwr and Dryslwyn in Wales were small boroughs beside castles. Torksey on the Trent had {{examples.Torksey.hh|int}} households in Domesday Book and a borough; its parish has {{examples.Torksey.pop|int}} residents today. You can find all of them, and look up any place near you, on the interactive map.

How I did this

I parsed the Gazetteer's county pages into derived variables only: counts, years, kinds of evidence, flags and converted coordinates, not its text. Its grid references are in hectometres on the National Grid; I converted them and checked {{grid_checks.n|int}} well-known places, all within 1 km of the town of that name, and corrected {{counts.grid_fixed|int}} clearly mistyped references. Places were matched to OS Open Names by name within 4 km ({{counts.name_matched_share|pct0}} found), populations come from Census 2021 built-up areas, and Domesday households from the Hull Domesday Project. "Town" here means what Ordnance Survey calls a town or city today, which is a cartographic label rather than a legal one, and a grant is not proof a market was ever held. Everything here is association, not cause.
